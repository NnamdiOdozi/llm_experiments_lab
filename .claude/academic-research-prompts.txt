
1. **Gap analysis**

> I’m working on [YOUR TOPIC].  
> Build a gap-analysis matrix from the literature I provide.  
> For each paper, extract:  
> research question / claim,  
> method,  
> dataset or experimental setting,  
> supervision/input assumptions,  
> baselines,  
> evaluation metrics,  
> reported limitations,  
> and what was not tested.  
>   
> Then identify candidate gaps in four categories:  
> methodological, empirical, evaluation, and assumption-level.  
>   
> For every proposed gap, show which papers establish it and distinguish:  
> (a) explicitly acknowledged gap,  
> (b) gap inferred from the comparison,  
> (c) idea not supported by the supplied literature.  
>   
> Do not claim novelty from absence alone.

2. **Stress-test my thinking**

> I’m investigating [YOUR RESEARCH QUESTION].  
> My current understanding is [YOUR REASONING / INTUITION].  
> Map the logic of this argument and challenge it.  
>   
> Identify:  
> assumptions I may be treating as established,  
> logical jumps,  
> missing variables or mechanisms,  
> alternative explanations,  
> concepts I may be conflating,  
> and relevant directions I may not have considered.  
>   
> For each issue, explain why it matters and what evidence would resolve it.  
>   
> Then suggest 2–3 technically plausible ways to extend or reframe the idea, but do not assume those suggestions are correct or novel.

3. **Look for contradictions**

> Audit my current research setup for internal inconsistencies.  
> I will provide:  
> research claim: [CLAIM]  
> method/architecture: [METHOD]  
> training setup: [SETUP]  
> evaluation: [EVALUATION]  
> results: [RESULTS]  
>   
> Build a contradiction and assumption table with:  
> Claim | Assumption | Evidence supporting it | Evidence contradicting it | Missing evidence | Severity | What must be checked  
>   
> Specifically test for:  
> train/test leakage,  
> mismatched baselines,  
> uncontrolled variables,  
> metric–claim mismatch,  
> architectural descriptions inconsistent with implementation,  
> conclusions stronger than the experiment,  
> conflicting labels/results,  
> and assumptions being treated as facts.  
>   
> Do not repair the problem yet. Diagnose it first.

4. **Rehearse research questions**

> I’m presenting/defending [YOUR STUDY]. My central claim is [CLAIM], and I’ll provide the method, experiments and results.  
>   
> Generate the questions a technically informed reviewer, advisor or committee member is most likely to ask.  
>   
> Include questions about:  
> why this research question matters,  
> why I chose this method rather than plausible alternatives,  
> what each major component contributes,  
> how I know the observed effect comes from the mechanism I claim,  
> unexpected or negative results,  
> limitations and boundary conditions,  
> interpretation of the results,  
> and what the work does not establish.  
>   
> For each question, return:  
> question + why they would ask it + evidence from my work needed to answer it + strongest answer supported by that evidence + follow-up question they could ask

5. **Refine writing without losing your voice**

> I’m writing about [YOUR TOPIC / RESULT].  
> Before rewriting anything, identify:  
> the exact claim this paragraph/section needs to establish,  
> the evidence that supports it,  
> any logical jump or overclaim,  
> terminology that must remain technically precise,  
> and anything that is repetitive, vague, or stronger than the results justify.  
>   
> Then revise the text while preserving:  
> my original argument,  
> technical meaning,  
> citation boundaries,  
> level of certainty,  
> and my writing style.  
>   
> Do not add new claims, citations, interpretations, or “academic-sounding” language that is not supported.  
>   
> If a sentence cannot be improved without changing its meaning, flag it instead of rewriting it.
